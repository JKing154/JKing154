Three-point bend test setup

Background

The steering wheel is the primary interface between the driver and the car. It has to fit within the front roll hoop clearance, accommodate drivers of different hand sizes, and hold up through an entire competition day without becoming a liability. Looking at the history of Cornell Racing steering wheels, each year brought a different approach. The ARG24 wheel became a fan favorite for its simplicity and comfort. Last year's design tried to integrate electronics and custom molded grips, which added complexity without a clear payoff. For ARG26, once it was decided that no buttons were needed while driving, I had a clean slate to focus purely on structural performance and driver comfort.

Design

The ARG26 steering wheel has three main components: a carbon fiber composite central panel, 3D printed grips, and a quick-disconnect. I wanted something simple and effective rather than overengineered. The grips were wrapped in Alcantara fabric for additional grip and cushioning, and attached to the panel with hardware. The quick-disconnect connects directly to the spline spigot on the column, with grease applied at the interface to address a slop issue from the prior year.

One mid-season iteration came from driver feedback — one driver reported that the lower cutout was too tight, limiting grip to only the thumb and pointer finger. I stretched the bottom of the wheel downward to open that space up, staying within front roll hoop clearance since the wheel never rotates more than 90 degrees.

Panel Testing and Material Selection

To select the right carbon fiber layup, I fabricated and tested four panel configurations using a three-point bend test in Bovay Lab. All panels used a 3-core-3 sandwich construction with a 0.5" core unless noted:

Panel 1 used a 0/45 ply schedule. Panel 2 used 0/45/0. Panel 3 used 0 only. Panel 4 used a 0/45/0 schedule with a thicker 0.75" core. Results showed that adding a single ply per side corresponded to roughly 100 lbf of additional yield strength at a cost of only 10 to 20 grams. Panel 2 was selected for the final design, delivering approximately 400 lbf yield strength at 50 grams — well above the 140 lbf recommended threshold while keeping the panel light. All four panels cleared the structural requirement, so the decision came down to the best strength-to-weight tradeoff rather than simply the strongest option.

One manufacturing issue during fabrication: an adhesive sheet was not fully removed from an adhesive ply during layup, which caused several panels to delaminate after curing. It went unnoticed until the panels came out of the oven. Working independently for the first time on a composite layup, that was a hard lesson in how much process discipline matters when you cannot visually inspect what is happening inside the bag.

Driver Feedback

Grip geometry was driven entirely by driver input. The Ergo subteam developed a musculoskeletal survey for drivers to complete after drive sessions, collecting numerical comfort ratings across body contact points to identify what needed to change. That data fed directly back into CAD iterations. The goal was not to design for one driver's hand but to find geometry that worked across the range of people who would be behind the wheel at competition.

The Problem

Spotted Lanternflies are devastating vineyards across the Northeast with no natural predators and limited effective control methods. They feed directly on grapevines, reducing yield and threatening plant health season after season. Our client, E&J Gallo Winery, needed a solution that could intercept SLFs before they ever reached the vines rather than dealing with the damage after the fact.

The Concept

We identified a critical behavioral weakness in the spotted lanternfly and exploited it. A USDA study found that SLFs are uniquely attracted to 60 Hz frequency stimuli. We adapted the proven lure-and-kill method of UV bug traps to that specific frequency, creating a passive trap that draws SLFs in and eliminates them on contact with an electrified dual-layer mesh. No pesticides, no manual removal, no ongoing maintenance beyond deployment.

Prototype and Build

The HertzTrap enclosure measures approximately 8 by 8 by 2 inches and was fabricated using 3D printing for rapid iteration and a rigid weatherproof housing. At the center sits a 57mm speaker driven by an Arduino Nano and amplifier circuit generating a continuous 60 Hz signal. Surrounding it is a dual-layer mesh system: a higher-density inner copper mesh and a lower-density outer metal mesh held at opposite polarities, powered by a repurposed handheld bug zapper circuit board running off two AA batteries. When an SLF bridges the gap between mesh layers it completes the circuit and is eliminated instantly. The outer mesh spacing was specifically sized so SLFs can physically bridge the two layers while keeping out debris. Four independently adjustable legs allow the trap to be staked and leveled at grapevine canopy height across uneven terrain.

Testing and Results

We validated three key performance criteria through physical testing. Structural durability was confirmed by applying incremental loads to the enclosure top — the trap withstood 40 lbs without deformation or failure, exceeding our 30 lb target, and we also dropped 5 to 10 lb weights from a foot high to simulate branches falling on it. Adjustability was verified by extending and locking each leg independently, confirming a maximum stable height of 3 feet near vine canopy level. Portability was tested by timing a single user relocating the trap across a simulated farm layout — consistently moved and reset in under 15 seconds, meaning one person could feasibly deploy over 100 units in a standard workday at 6 lbs total weight.

Successful arc discharge between the mesh layers was confirmed during bench testing, validating the electrical system functions at the chosen mesh spacing. The honest limitation is that biological testing with live insects was not conducted, so kill reliability and attraction range under real field conditions remain the most important unknowns for future development.

Outcome

The HertzTrap won People's Choice out of the entire MAE 2250 class, voted on by peers and faculty. Future development would focus on solar power integration for continuous field operation, a custom compact circuit board, and controlled field experiments to quantify attraction range and capture rate with live insects in a vineyard environment.

E-Bike Frame — Ansys FEA & Design Iteration

Structural Mechanics Simulation (FEA) Final Project · Cornell
TOOLAnsys Workbench, shell elements
VERIFICATIONMesh convergence study
FINDING29 → 56 MPa true stress after convergence
RESULTStep-through redesign, SF > 2

The Setup

Structural analysis of an e-bike frame for bike-share use, modeled as thin-walled tubes using shell elements rather than solids — more accurate in bending for thin sections and far cheaper to solve. Loads included rider forces at the seat and pedals plus a 3 kg battery mass, with aluminum alloy material properties.

Why the First Answer Was Wrong

The initial 4 mm mesh reported a peak von Mises stress of 29 MPa at the bottom bracket junction. Rather than accept that, I ran a mesh convergence study — and the stress nearly doubled, converging around 56 MPa. The initial mesh was significantly under-predicting stress at the sharp tube intersections. It's the clearest lesson FEA teaches: an unconverged result isn't a result, and the solver will happily hand you a wrong answer with full confidence.

Redesign

I then modified the geometry into a step-through frame (no top tube) for accessibility, predicting the stress would migrate into the long curved tubes to the head tube. The simulation confirmed the prediction, with stresses concentrating at tube junctions as expected, and the final design passed the required safety factor of 2 under full rider and battery loading.

Assembled pocket AI assistant

Built a pocket-sized AI voice assistant using a Raspberry Pi Zero 2W, a Whisplay audio HAT, and a PiSugar 3 battery so it runs fully untethered. The goal was a device that works without any subscription or cloud dependency — everything either runs locally on the Pi or hits a free API tier.

Speech recognition uses faster-whisper running locally (tiny model to stay within the Pi Zero's limited RAM), the language model runs through Groq's API for speed, and text-to-speech uses Piper locally. The main remaining challenge is response latency — getting the full pipeline fast enough to feel natural to talk to.

2013 BMW 335i — F30 N55

Working on the 335i is less about following a guide and more about actually diagnosing what is wrong before touching anything. The N55 is well documented but it still throws problems that need real troubleshooting — scanning codes, pulling live data, and understanding what the symptom is actually pointing to before ordering a single part. Half the job is not assuming you know the answer.

Work done so far: replaced the charge pipe, which is a known weak point on the N55 that cracks under boost pressure and causes boost leaks. The rear brake light board was fried and needed to be swapped out. Diagnosed and resolved ABS faults. Replaced a faulty parking sensor. Tracked down and addressed an interior water leak. Currently working through a drivetrain shudder under load, which is still being diagnosed. Next up is getting ahead of things with preventative maintenance before problems show up rather than after.

1990 Volkswagen Corrado — Restoration

The Corrado is a longer term project. This 1990 Volkswagen has traveled with my dad around England, France, Martinique, and now Watertown. It has not driven for 7 years since my dad fell ill so it needs some TLC. The car has been sitting and needs serious work before it is roadworthy again. The plan is a full fuel system cleanout, timing belt replacement, and adding a resonator. It is the kind of project where you pull one thing apart and find three more things that need attention, which is part of what makes it worthwhile. Working through it to have my dad ride in it once again is the final goal.
